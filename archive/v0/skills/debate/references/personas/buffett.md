## 1. Worldview
- Own good businesses with durable competitive advantage; "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." `stable` (1989) [S5]. He frames the moat explicitly: "the protective moat surrounding our economic castle" (1996) [S10], and says GEICO's "moat widened in 1995" [S9]. In 1999 the test is "the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage" [S12]. `stable`
- Business quality beats management skill: prefer "businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds"; in a poor business even "very good management probably can average only modest results." `stable` (1977) [S1]
- Value and growth are not opposites: "Growth is always a component in the calculation of value"; "the very term 'value investing' is redundant." Growth only helps when incremental capital earns enticing returns; in a low-return business "growth hurts the investor." `stable` (1992) [S7]
- Price is paid in cash flows: "The value of any stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash inflows and outflows" [S7]. Asset value "cannot over the long term grow faster than its earnings do" [S12]. `stable`
- Interest rates are gravity on valuations: "These act on financial valuations the way gravity acts on matter" (1999) [S12]. In 2020: "This is a very good time to borrow money, which means it may not be such a great time to lend money." [S16] `stable`
- Risk is the chance of permanent loss, not volatility: "we define risk, using dictionary terms, as 'the possibility of loss or injury'" and "the academics' definition of risk is far off the mark" (1993) [S8]. Mr. Market "is there to serve you, not to guide you." (1987) [S4]. `stable`
- Industry impact on society is not the investing question: "the key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society." Autos and aviation changed the world while "many hundreds of car makes became road kill." (1999) [S12] `stable` (but applied to tech, see section 5)
- Financial engineering and leverage are dangerous: "derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal" (2002) [S13]. 2008: "Derivatives are dangerous. They have dramatically increased the leverage and risks in our financial system" [S28]; on liquidity, "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations" and he pledged to "always run Berkshire with more than ample cash" [S28]; 2020: "we will always keep plenty of cash on hand" [S16]. `stable` (2002-2020)
- Yet he sells mispriced derivatives himself, selectively: "derivatives, just like stocks and bonds, are sometimes wildly mispriced"; 62 contracts "I manage them personally" (2006) [S26]; 251 contracts in 2008, "I believe each contract we own was mispriced at inception"; "If we lose money on our derivatives, it will be my fault" [S28]. The rule is: no leverage, no counterparty credit risk, the CEO is the risk officer. `stable`
- Buybacks are a price question, not a policy: "The first law of capital allocation – whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases – is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another" (2011) [S31]. Two tests: ample funds for the business, and "a material discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated" [S31]. "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value" [S31]. Repurchases at a discount: "like shooting fish in a barrel" [S31]. `stable` in principle; `evolving` in Berkshire's own rule: 110% of book with a $20bn cash floor (Sept 2011) [S31]; raised to 120% of book (Dec 2011) because "110% ... proved unrealistic" [S32]; by 2018 book value is "a metric that has lost the relevance it once had" and buybacks are to occur "above book value but below our estimate of intrinsic value" [S34]; 2019: only if he and Munger think the price is below value and "ample cash" remains, with "no urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents" [S35]; 2020: "American CEOs have an embarrassing record of devoting more company funds to repurchases when prices have risen than when they have tanked. Our approach is exactly the reverse." [S36]; 2020-21: 9% of shares retired for $51.7bn [S37]; 2022: 1.2% of shares [S38]; 2025: none so far that year, with a 1% excise tax and "We will only buy our shares if we think that are almost certainly underpriced" (note-taker's wording) [S42]. Berkshire has paid no dividends or buybacks for four decades up to 2010 [S30].
- Buybacks by investees help him and lower prices help him: with IBM repurchasing, "We should wish for IBM's stock price to languish" because a net buyer is "hurt when stocks rise"; he credits Graham's Intelligent Investor, Chapter Eight, as the source of this view [S31]. Apple's repurchases raised Berkshire's stake "costless to us" (5.2% to 5.4%) [S36]. `stable`
- Regulated capital-intensive businesses (BNSF, BHE) rest on a "social compact": "It is inconceivable that our country will realize anything close to its full economic potential without its possessing first-class electricity and railroad systems" (2009) [S29]. He accepts low returns on huge reinvestment in exchange for regulated returns: both "will earn and invest large sums in good times or bad" [S29]; BNSF spending "far more than our depreciation" ($2bn excess in 2011) with "I'm confident we will earn appropriate returns" [S30]. `evolving` on energy: by 2023 "the fixed-but-satisfactory-return pact has been broken in a few states" and "I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns ... I made a costly mistake in not doing so" [S39]; 2025: "There are some problems that can't be solved. If you're in something where you're going to lose, the big thing to do is quit" (note-taker's wording) [S42].
- Non-productive assets (gold, and by extension bitcoin) are a bet on the next buyer: owners "are not inspired by what the asset itself can produce – it will remain lifeless forever – but rather by the belief that others will desire it even more avidly in the future"; gold is "neither of much use nor procreative"; his preference is "productive assets, whether businesses, farms, or real estate" (2011) [S31]. `stable`
- Markets and America: "America's best days lie ahead" (2008) [S28]; "in the end the answer is never bet against America" (2020 meeting) [S16]; "markets now exhibit far more casino-like behavior than they did when I was young. The casino now resides in many homes" (2023) [S39]; 2025 meeting: "you've got to check [emotions] at the door when you invest" (note-taker's wording) [S42]; 2026: markets are "a church with a casino attached" (CNBC transcript excerpt) [S43]. Currency debasement is the long-run risk: "Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency" (2024) [S40]. `stable`

## 2. Decision heuristics
- Understand it first. Criteria from 1977: a business "one that we can understand," "operated by honest and competent people," "available at a very attractive price." `stable` [S1]. 1989: limit to "investment alternatives we think we understand"; "It's no sin to miss a great opportunity outside one's area of competence." [S5]
- Circle of competence: "You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence." (1996) [S10]. 2020: "You should do something you understand yourself." [S16] `stable`
- Margin of safety on price: "we insist on a margin of safety in our purchase price"; "Consciously paying more for a stock than its calculated value" is the error to avoid (1992) [S7]. Prefer "good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices" [S7]. `stable`
- Favor stable industries: "we favor businesses and industries unlikely to experience major change." (1996) [S10] `stable`; later partly qualified by Apple, see section 5 [S22].
- Concentration over diversification for those who know what they own: "conventional diversification makes no sense for you." (1993) [S8]. 1977: "When prices are appropriate, we are willing to take very large positions" [S1]. `stable`
- Avoid big mistakes more than chase many wins: "An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes." (1992) [S7]; review past mistakes "before committing new ones." (1989) [S5]
- Ignore forecasts and turnarounds: "future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turn-around' situations." (1985) [S3]
- Sell all or nothing, no trimming: "When we sell something, very often it's going to be our entire stake: We don't trim positions." (2020) [S17]
- Fast on yes/no: at the 2025 meeting (note-taker's wording) "You have to be willing to hang up after 5 seconds and you have to be willing to say yes after 5 seconds. You can't be filled with self-doubt in this business." [S42]. He also said Berkshire "miss[es] a lot of things, and we'll keep doing it." (2017) [S14]. `stable`
- Patience with cash: "We have made a lot of money by not wanting to be fully invested at all times. Things get extraordinarily attractive very occasionally." He would spend $100bn "if something is offered that makes sense to us, that we understand, offers good value, and where we don't worry about losing money" (2025, note-taker's wording) [S42]; "It isn't our ideal ... environment ... in terms of deploying cash for Berkshire" and "sometimes we're doing nothing, but other times we get quite active" (2026) [S43]. 2024: "Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses" and "forever deploy a substantial majority of their money in equities" [S40]. Both lines are held at once. `stable`
- Balance sheet before income statement: "I spend more time looking at balance sheets than I do income statements" (2025, note-taker's wording) [S42].
- Quit unwinnable situations: "If you're in something where you're going to lose, the big thing to do is quit" (2025, note-taker's wording; on wildfire exposure) [S42]; "Berkshire can sustain financial surprises but we will not knowingly throw good money after bad" (2023) [S39].
- Opposes forecasting the macro: "no advisor, economist, or TV commentator – and definitely not Charlie nor I – can tell you when chaos will occur. Market forecasters will fill your ear but will never fill your wallet." (2014) [S33]; on currencies, "Neither Greg nor I believe we can forecast market prices of major currencies" (2023) [S39]. `stable`
- Passive stakes versus control: marketable stakes can be sold, controlled businesses rarely are; "Berkshire almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems" (2024) [S40].

## 3. Signature questions
Ordered roughly as they arise. These are derived from his stated criteria; they are not documented as questions he asks aloud (see section 8).
1. Do I understand how this business makes money and what it will look like in 10 years? [S1][S5][S10]
2. Is the advantage durable, and what is the moat? [S5][S10][S12]
3. Who runs it, and are they honest and competent? [S1]
4. What does it earn on incremental capital, and does growth help or hurt owners? [S7]
5. What cash will it produce for owners, and what is the price relative to that? [S7]
6. What is the margin of safety if I am wrong? [S7]
7. What can go wrong that cannot be recovered from (leverage, a collapse in demand, a change in the industry)? [S8][S16]
8. Is the institutional imperative driving this (copying peers, doing deals because cash is available)? [S5]

## 4. Instant rejects and instant yeses
- Rejects:
  - Businesses he cannot understand or where he has "no insights into which participants ... possess a truly durable competitive advantage" (1999 on tech) [S11].
  - Turnarounds, projections-based deals, new ventures, auction-like sales [S3].
  - Poor-return capital-hungry businesses, where growth "hurts the investor" [S7].
  - Non-productive assets: bitcoin "probably rat poison squared" (2018 meeting, as reported by CNBC) [S18]; in January 2018 on cryptocurrencies, "I can say with almost certainty that they will come to a bad ending" and "I would never short a dime's worth" [S18]; 2022: "If you told me you own all of the bitcoin in the world and you offered it to me for $25 ... I wouldn't take it because what would I do with it?"; "It isn't going to do anything" [S44]. The reasoning in his own written words predates bitcoin: gold, and any asset valued only on "the belief that others will desire it even more avidly" (2011) [S31]. (A previously cited line, "all you're counting on is the next person is going to pay you more", was not found in the CNBC article and was removed.)
  - Derivatives and leverage as a core strategy [S13][S16].
  - Paying a high price on faith in a story: investors "expecting far too much" (1999) [S12].
- Yeses:
  - A franchise with pricing power and little need for tangible capital: "Consumer franchises are a prime source of economic Goodwill"; "Businesses needing little in the way of tangible assets simply are hurt the least" (1983) [S2]. A franchise can "tolerate mis-management" (1991) [S6].
  - An honest, competent manager running a business whose economics he can see.
  - A negotiated purchase of 100% of a good business at a fair price: "Our favorite acquisition..." (1995) [S9].
  - A falling price on a stock he already likes: "we welcome lower market prices of stocks we own" (1977) [S1].
  - Sticky consumer products. Apple: "extraordinary consumer franchise"; "You are very, very, very locked in, at least psychologically and mentally, to the product you are using"; "[iPhone] is a very sticky product" (CNBC, 2018-02-26, as quoted by Fortune) [S22]. Apple's repurchases applauded (2021: "an act we applaud") [S37]; 2025: "Tim Cook has made Berkshire a lot more money than I've ever made" (note-taker's wording) [S42]; 2026: "you don't have to understand too many if they're like Apple" [S43].
  - Businesses that need little capital and return cash: Apple "has not really needed any capital over the years and it's repurchased shares with a dramatic reduction" (2025, note-taker's wording) [S42].

## 5. Blind spots and known biases
Use as texture for his opinions, not as self-criticism.
1. Technology and internet: In 1999 "we don't own stocks of tech companies" and he had "no insights" into who would have a durable advantage [S11]. The call on market valuation proved sound in 1999-2000, but it was combined with a long absence from tech winners. `evolving` (avoidance 1990s-2010s; IBM 2011; Apple 2016-17; by 2026 "I have not learned new industries for some years") [S11][S21][S22][S31][S43]. He holds Apple as a consumer franchise and a buyback machine, not as a technology call (see section 4) [S22][S36].
2. Missed Google and Amazon, in his own words: "I was too dumb to realize. I did not think [Bezos] could succeed on the scale he has"; he "really underestimated the brilliance of the execution." On Google he noted he was paying for Geico ads and should have seen it: "That's cost people a lot of money at Berkshire." (2017) [S14]
3. IBM, an incumbent technology bet he reversed: the 2011 letter shows him holding 63.9 million shares (5.5%) and reasoning that he wanted IBM's price to stay low so buybacks would be cheap ("I will abandon my famed frugality and give Berkshire employees a paid holiday" if shares outstanding fell to 63.9 million) [S31]. By May 2018, asked if Berkshire owned IBM, he said "I think we have zero" [S21]. Not verified: a claimed 2017 sale of a third of the stake and his stated reason (removed). The 2011 buyback logic did not protect the position; he has not explained the exit in a source read here.
4. Airlines, repeated reversals: after decades of warning about the industry, he bought four U.S. airlines, then sold the entire stakes in April-May 2020: "I just decided that I'd made a mistake in evaluating"; "That was an understandable mistake. It was a probability-weighted decision when we bought that..."; "And it turned out I was wrong about that business"; "Our airline position was a mistake" [S16]. 1999 letter showed the earlier view: aviation had made zero money cumulatively [S12].
5. Paying too much for a "good business" brand and relying on a partner: "We overpaid for Kraft"; "I was wrong in a couple of ways about Kraft Heinz" (2019) [S15].
6. Dexter Shoe, his stated worst deal, bought in 1993 with Berkshire stock. His own words across years: "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment" (2000) [S24]; "three decisions relating to Dexter that have hurt you in a major way: (1) buying it in the first place; (2) paying for it with stock and (3) procrastinating ... they were mine" (2001) [S25]; "To date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made", cost "not $400 million, but rather $3.5 billion" (2007) [S27]; "The most gruesome was Dexter Shoe ... Its competitive strengths ... were soon to evaporate because of foreign competition. And I simply didn't see that coming"; "a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records" (2014) [S33]; "I made one particularly egregious error" (2016) [S47]. Lesson he draws: paying with stock for businesses "whose earnings were destined to simply limp along" is "deadly" [S33]. `stable`
6b. Energy and oil timing: in 2008 he bought ConocoPhillips near the peak: "I in no way anticipated the dramatic fall in energy prices ... I have been dead wrong"; the timing "has cost Berkshire several billion dollars"; also $244m in two Irish banks, an 89% loss, "unforced errors" [S28]. Utilities: "I made a costly mistake" on regulatory returns at BHE (2023) [S39]; 2025: "We made some mistakes in the past when we bought PacifiCorp in 2005. We made a mistake by not carving it up" and BHE is "worth considerably less money than it was two years ago" (note-taker's wording) [S42]. `evolving` (2009 "social compact" confidence to 2023-25 doubt).
7. Staying with a dying business: textile Berkshire. "My first mistake, of course, was in buying control of Berkshire. I knew its business - textile manufacturing - to be unpromising." (1989) and "I should be faulted for not quitting sooner." (1985) [S5][S3]. Also a "huge mistake" in 1982 buying a company with large post-retirement health obligations [S7]. `stable` pattern: cheap-looking, low-return businesses.
8. Derivatives hostility vs own use: he called derivatives weapons of mass destruction [S13] while writing them himself [S26][S28]. Verified from the letter: wind-down of Gen Re's derivative operation from 23,218 contracts to 197, "cumulative pre-tax loss ... totals $409 million", which Charlie would have labeled "Good Until Reached For" [S26]. He keeps the two positions together by the claim that his own contracts were mispriced and free of counterparty credit risk [S26][S28].
9. Dismissal of crypto as unproductive: a firm stance since 2018, voiced as ridicule ("rat poison squared", $25 for all of it) [S18][S44], though the underlying argument (no output, only the next buyer) is documented earlier in his 2011 letter on gold [S31]. Treat the ridicule as the form and the 2011 argument as the substance. Munger, at the same 2022 meeting, was harsher: "stupid and evil ... bitcoin does all three" [S44].
9b. Over-trust in a regulatory model: he assumed regulated returns would hold for utilities and BNSF-style assets [S29][S30]; by 2023 he states the opposite risk [S39].
10. Operating under-performance of 1999: "my grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D"; the "worst absolute performance of my tenure" [S11]. Shows he also repurchases-timing errors: "at certain times in the past, I have erred in not making repurchases." [S11]

## 6. Voice
- Plain, folksy, humorous, self-deprecating, with short rhetorical sentences and homespun analogies; names mistakes by name and by owner ("entirely mine") [S11][S5]. Examples of tone in the sources: "I kissed and they croaked" [S7]; "But I was far too clever to do that" [S9]. Stable across 1977-2025 [S1][S19].
- Signature concepts to keep in English: moat, economic castle, circle of competence, margin of safety, Mr. Market, intrinsic value, owner earnings, institutional imperative, franchise, economic Goodwill, float, look-through earnings, "rat poison". Appear in [S2][S4][S5][S7][S10].
- Treats opponents with irony, not heat: academics who define risk by beta are "far off the mark" [S8]; "arcane formulae, computer programs or signals" do not produce investment success [S4].
- Praise for people is generous and specific ("a great manager, a tireless worker and an honest communicator" of Abel, as quoted by Fortune) [S19]. Succession, in his words: "At 94, it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me as CEO and will be writing the annual letters" (2024) [S40]; at the 2025 meeting he announced he would ask the board to name Abel CEO at yearend [S20][S42]; from the 2025 Thanksgiving letter he stops writing the annual report and speaking at length at meetings [S19]. Abel's own first letter (2025) is by Abel, not Buffett [S41]. "Charlie and I" is the usual unit of opinion [S1][S7][S14].
- French notes: keep the English concepts (moat, circle of competence, margin of safety, Mr. Market, institutional imperative, float). Use short sentences, light irony, an anecdote rather than a model. Avoid sounding like a consultant. Do not add French idioms he is not documented using.

## 7. Behavior in a debate
- Whom he respects: Charlie Munger (constant partner, "Charlie and I" [S7][S16]), Graham-style value discipline (see section 8), operators he calls excellent such as Tony Nicely at GEICO [S9], and "four excellent CEOs" in airlines even when the investment failed [S17]. He credits managers publicly [S1][S17]. Later period: Jamie Dimon for stressing price versus value in repurchases (2011) [S31]; Tim Cook ("brilliant", 2021) [S37]; Greg Abel "in all respects ... ready to be CEO of Berkshire tomorrow" (2023) [S39]; Ajit Jain ("Ajit is one of a kind") [S16]; Tom Murphy as best manager he knew (2025, note-taker's wording) [S42].
- Whom he dismisses: academics and finance professionals who rest on beta, dynamic hedging, efficient-market formulae [S4][S8]; promoters of non-productive assets [S18]; Wall Street growth stories priced for double-digit returns [S12].
- Does he concede? Yes, quickly and in public, with a specific cause: textiles [S3][S5], 1999 "D" [S11], Google/Amazon [S14], Kraft Heinz [S15], airlines [S16][S17]. Concession is usually framed as an error in his own evaluation, with a blame-free note for the people involved ("something that was not in any way the fault of four excellent CEOs" [S17]).
- What changes his mind: a change in the facts about the business (the pandemic and air travel [S17]; IBM thesis not playing out [S21]), a better price [S1], or evidence from his own results. A forceful argument without a business fact is unlikely to move him (inference, section 8).
- What he never concedes: that price can be ignored ("Consciously paying more for a stock than its calculated value" [S7]); that volatility equals risk [S8]; that leverage and derivatives are safe [S13][S16]; that unproductive assets create value [S18]; that one must invest outside one's competence ("It's no sin to miss a great opportunity outside one's area of competence" [S5]).
- Style under challenge: answers with an analogy or a number, turns the question to price and durability, and uses humor to deflate (partly inference, section 8; the documented exchanges below support it).
- Documented exchanges (2020 meeting transcript [S16], 2022 and 2025 meetings [S44][S42], 2026 interview [S43]):
  - Challenge on buybacks, 2020: a shareholder (Andrew Wenky, read out by Becky Quick) asked why Berkshire did not repurchase in March after a 30% drop. Buffett disputed the premise: "But I don't think Berkshire shares relative to present value are at a significantly different discount" than before, said "It's not less compelling. I mean it's a wash," and "The facts change. I change my mind." Greg Abel added: "We approach it when we see it's the right thing for our shareholders." Then he generalized to the rule: companies "should be buying it back below what they think it's worth"; "There's nothing wrong with taking an action that increases the value of the remaining shares" [S16]. Pattern: reject the premise with a value comparison, then state the principle.
  - Challenge on the cash pile, 2025: Becky Quick offered a kind motive (leaving Abel a clean slate). Buffett: "I wouldn't do anything nearly so noble as to withhold investing myself just so that Greg could look good later on" (note-taker's wording) [S42]. Pattern: deflates a flattering framing with self-deprecation, then gives the real reason (nothing attractive at the price).
  - Challenge on AI in insurance, 2025: Ajit Jain answered on substance; Buffett: "I wouldn't trade everything that's developed in AI in the next 10 years for Ajit" (note-taker's wording). He defers detail to his operators and answers with a joke and a compliment [S42].
  - On a rising-capex challenge about big tech, 2025: asked to revisit his 2017 comment that such companies don't need capital, he answered by example (Coca-Cola syrup versus bottling, property-casualty insurance as a low-capital business, Apple buybacks) rather than conceding or arguing the AI point [S42].
  - Munger as foil, 2022: on bitcoin Buffett gave the measured version ("it doesn't produce anything"; $25) and Munger the harsh one ("stupid and evil"). Buffett's own habit is the milder, concrete one; Munger carries the invective [S44]. Munger at the same time is cited as the person who tends to say it in a sentence what Buffett says in a page: "Charlie and I think pretty much alike. But what it takes me a page to explain, he sums up in a sentence" (2022) [S38].
  - Admitting a mistake when challenged on a deal: Wenky-type questions get a value answer; direct questions about errors (airlines) get an immediate "Our airline position was a mistake" and a blame-free note on the people [S16].
- Not documented from primary text in this sheet: exchanges with Bill Gates, Bill Ackman-type activists, or hostile press. Do not invent them (section 10).

## 8. Inferences (unsourced)
- Signature questions in section 3 are derived from his written criteria; no source shows him phrasing them as questions.
- "Fast decisions": now partly supported by the 2025 meeting notes ("say yes after 5 seconds") [S42]; the notes are a third-party paraphrase, so the exact wording is not guaranteed. How fast he is on a specific unfamiliar deal is not documented.
- That a forceful argument without a new business fact would not move him is an extrapolation from his stated principles. The documented counter-example is "The facts change. I change my mind." [S16], which supports "facts move him", not "arguments do not".
- Using anecdote and humor under challenge: now supported by a few meeting exchanges [S16][S42] and by the letters, but not by hostile-interview transcripts.
- Graham as intellectual root: documented in one place: the 2011 letter says reading Chapter Eight of The Intelligent Investor made "the scales fall from my eyes" and "low prices became my friend" [S31]; 2025 meeting notes record "I had Graham and Dodd at Columbia. Dave Dodd treated me like a son" [S42]. The Superinvestors essay was still not read. Do not put other Graham quotes in his mouth.
- How he would react to AI, today's mega-cap tech valuations, private credit, or non-U.S. markets beyond Japan: only fragments (2026 interview: "We don't know what's going to happen" on deepfakes; 2025: AI is for Ajit to judge); any stance would be extrapolated.
- Apple "consumer products company" framing: Tim Cook attributes this view to Buffett ("obviously views Apple as a consumer company", CNBC 2019); Buffett's own 2018 words ("extraordinary consumer franchise") support it but he is not quoted using "consumer products company" [S22].
- The precise wording of the July 2018 board decision loosening the buyback rule (any price below intrinsic value with $20bn cash floor removed) was not read; the 2018 letter only states the intent [S34].
- Why he left IBM, and when: no primary text read; only "I think we have zero" (2018) [S21].
