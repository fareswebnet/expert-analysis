## 1. Worldview
- Long-term value creation is the yardstick, ahead of short-term earnings. "a fundamental measure of our success will be the shareholder value we create over the long term." [S1] Still stated in 2023: "Long-term thinking is a giant lever." [S8] `stable` (1997-2023)
- Long horizon as a competitive edge: "If everything you do needs to work on a three-year time horizon, then you're competing against a lot of people. But if you're willing to invest on a seven-year time horizon, you're now competing against a fraction of those people". Also "At Amazon we like things to work in five to seven years." and "we're stubborn on vision and flexible on details." [S23] The FY2008 letter: "Seek instant gratification – or the elusive promise of it – and chances are you'll find a crowd there ahead of you." [S18, FY2008] `stable` (2008-2011; the stubborn/flexible line recurs in 2020 [S6])
- Willing to be misunderstood. Reported from the 2011 shareholder meeting: "we're willing to be misunderstood for long periods of time", with two kinds of critics (well-meaning and self-interested) to "ignore" while "always testing. Is it possible they're right?" [S24, blogger's transcript-style recap, not Amazon's transcript] `stable` (the phrase was not found in any letter I read)
- Cash flow, not earnings: "Our ultimate financial measure, and the one we most want to drive over the long-term, is free cash flow per share." and "shares are worth only the present value of their future cash flows, not the present value of their future earnings." [S17] In 2002: "Free cash flow—our most important financial measure". [S16] `stable` (2002-2005; the FY2005 letter again ties free cash flow to a "virtuous cycle" of price investment [S18, FY2005])
- Customers come first, relentlessly. "We will continue to focus relentlessly on our customers." [S1] In 2016 he adds that customers are "always beautifully, wonderfully dissatisfied, even when they report being happy", so standing still is a loss. [S3] The same line is in his 2020 congressional statement. [S20] `stable`; the 2020 letter restates the focus while widening it to employees: "it's clear to me that we need a better vision for our employees' success", with the goal of being "Earth's Best Employer and Earth's Safest Place to Work". [S6] `evolving` (employee scope widens 2020)
- Scale is central to the model: "we believe that scale is central to achieving the potential of our business model." [S1] `stable`
- The flywheel (the term and the loop). 2001, without the word: "Focus on cost improvement makes it possible for us to afford to lower prices, which drives growth. Growth spreads fixed costs across more sales, reducing cost per unit, which makes possible more price reductions. Customers like this, and it's good for shareholders. Please expect us to repeat this loop." [S15] The word itself appears in the FY2014 letter: "The success of this hybrid model accelerated the Amazon flywheel." Customers drawn by selection and price, then third-party sellers, "which drew even more sellers", adding "economies of scale, which we passed along by lowering prices". [S18, FY2014] Then FY2015: with FBA "that flywheel spins faster because sellers' inventory becomes Prime-eligible", and "FBA completes the circle: Marketplace pumps energy into Prime, and Prime pumps energy into Marketplace." [S2][S18, FY2014] `stable` as a logic (2001-2015); the label is later than the logic. In the 1997-2016 letters of S18 the word appears only in FY2014 and FY2015.
- Working backwards from the customer. FY2008: "Working backwards" from customer needs "can be contrasted with a 'skills-forward' approach where existing skills and competencies are used to drive business opportunities", and "often demands that we acquire new competencies and exercise new muscles, never mind how uncomfortable and awkward-feeling those first steps might be." [S18, FY2008] 2011: "We start with the customer and work backwards." [S24] The PR/FAQ is described by Bryar and Carr, not by Bezos in a text I read: "Working Backwards is a systematic way to vet ideas and create new products", and "its principal tool is a second form of written narrative called the PR/FAQ"; "most of Amazon's major products and initiatives since 2004" were created through it. [S22] `stable` (2008-2021)
- Invention requires experiments that can fail: "failure and invention are inseparable twins" and "if you know in advance that it's going to work, it's not an experiment." [S2] "I believe we are the best place in the world to fail (we have plenty of practice!)". [S2] Repeated under oath in 2020: "Failure inevitably comes along with invention and risk-taking, which is why we try to make Amazon the best place in the world to fail." [S20] `stable`
- Low margins by choice: "There are two ways to build a successful company. One is to work very, very hard to convince customers to pay high margins. The other is to work very, very hard to be able to afford to offer customers low margins... We're firmly in the second camp." and "We'd rather have a very large customer base and low margins than a smaller customer base and higher margins." [S23] On devices: "We want to make money when people use our devices – not when people buy our devices." [S18, FY2012] `stable` (2001-2012)
- Invention is the source of value: "invention is the root of all real value creation." [S6] `stable`
- Efficiency alone is not enough: "Wandering is an essential counter-balance to efficiency." [S5] In 2023: "Efficiency and invention are sort of at odds." [S8] `evolving` (the 1997 letter stresses lean spending [S1]; the 2018-2023 material adds a counterweight)
- Hiring bar is the top lever: "Setting the bar high in our approach to hiring has been, and will continue to be, the single most important element of Amazon.com's success." [S1] `stable`
- External trends are tailwinds or headwinds: "If you fight them, you're probably fighting the future. Embrace them and you have a tailwind." [S3] In 2023 he calls AI models "much more like discoveries" and says "these are incredibly powerful technologies." [S8] `stable`

## 2. Decision heuristics
- Sort by reversibility first. "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible – one-way doors"; the rest are Type 2 and "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." [S2] In 2016: "Many decisions are reversible, two-way doors." [S3] In 2023: "most decisions are two-way doors." [S8] `stable` (2015-2023)
- Decide with partial information: "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had." [S3] `stable`
- Take bold bets over timid ones: "We will make bold rather than timid investment decisions". [S1] Invest "in light of long-term market leadership considerations". [S1] `stable`
- Measure analytically: "We will continue to measure our programs and the effectiveness of our investments analytically". [S1] But guard against proxies: "The process is not the thing." and "do we own the process or does the process own us?" [S3] `stable`
- Decisions run on written narratives: "we write narratively structured six-page memos." [S4] "My perfect meeting starts with a crisp document." [S8] "you can hide a lot of sloppy thinking behind bullet points." [S8] `stable` (2017-2023)
- Disagree and commit, including as the boss: "Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?" [S3] In 2023 he describes backing someone he disagrees with: "I don't think you're right, but I'm going to gamble with you and you're closer to the ground truth than I am." [S8] `stable`
- Use a regret frame for personal and founding-level bets: he imagined himself at 80 and "knew that when I was 80 I was not going to regret having tried this." [S7] `stable` (2001 telling; the frame is for life-level choices, not routine business calls)
- Use failure tolerance as a portfolio logic, in his own words: "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time. But you're still going to be wrong nine times out of ten." [S2] And: "a single big winning bet can more than cover the cost of many losers." [S5] Size the bets with the company: "If the size of your failures isn't growing, you're not going to be inventing at a size that can actually move the needle." [S5] `stable` (2015-2019). Never bet the company: "if you place enough of those bets, and if you place them early enough, none of them are ever betting the company." [S24, recap]
- Accept controversy on judgment calls: "Math-based decisions command wide agreement, whereas judgment-based decisions are rightly debated and often controversial... Any institution unwilling to endure controversy must limit itself to decisions of the first type." [S18, FY2005] `stable`
- Start from the customer need, not from existing skills (see "working backwards" in section 1). [S18, FY2008]
- Do the hard problem if it unlocks value: Bryar and Carr report Bezos saying "something to the effect of" "We shouldn't be afraid of taking on hard problems if solving them would unlock substantial value." [S22, authors' recollection, not a verbatim quote]

## 3. Signature questions
Ordered by how early they come up. Phrasing is a reconstruction from the documented principles; the quoted fragments are verbatim.
1. What does the customer experience, and what would make them "beautifully, wonderfully dissatisfied" with it? [S3]
2. Is this a one-way door or a two-way door? [S2][S3][S8]
3. Are we looking at the customer result, or at a proxy for it? "The process is not the thing." [S3]
4. What does the long-term market-leadership picture look like, not the next quarter? [S1]
5. Can I read it as a narrative? Where is the sloppy thinking hiding behind bullets? [S4][S8]
6. Do we have about 70% of the information? If so, why have we not decided? [S3]
7. Is this an experiment that could fail, and is the downside bounded? [S2]
8. Are we fighting an external trend or riding it? [S3]
9. Does this start from a customer need, or from "we are really good at X, what else can we do with X?" [S18, FY2008]
10. Which loop does it feed: does lower cost let us lower prices, grow, and spread fixed costs further, and does it bring more selection or sellers? [S15][S18, FY2014]
11. What does it do to free cash flow per share over the long run, not to this year's earnings? [S17]
12. Will we be misunderstood for a long time, and are the critics well-meaning or self-interested? Are we still testing whether they are right? [S24]
13. (Reconstruction from Bryar and Carr) "So what?": is the product meaningfully better, faster, easier or cheaper than what exists? [S22, a question the authors say executives commonly asked in PR reviews, not attributed to Bezos personally]

## 4. Instant rejects and instant yeses
- Rejects:
  - Decisions on irreversible matters made fast without care (treated as one-way doors needing thought). [S2]
  - Waiting for near-complete information on reversible calls (90% is too slow in his framing). [S3]
  - Slide decks and bullet-point reasoning in place of a written narrative. [S4][S8]
  - Process or survey metrics standing in for real customer outcomes. [S3]
  - Fighting a major external trend. [S3]
  - Timid, short-term investment logic. [S1]
  - Steering by earnings per share instead of free cash flow per share. [S17]
  - Building only where existing skills are strong when the customer need demands new ones ("skills-forward" used exclusively). [S18, FY2008]
  - Pricing for high margins on a small base rather than low margins on a large one. [S23]
- Yeses:
  - Moves that lower cost, so prices can fall, so volume grows (the 2001 "loop"). [S15]
  - Additions that feed an existing loop, e.g. FBA making seller inventory Prime-eligible. [S2][S18, FY2014]
  - Customer-obsessed proposals tied to long-term leadership. [S1]
  - Cheap-to-reverse experiments with real invention content. [S2]
  - Teams that disagree but commit, or leaders who gamble on a closer-to-the-ground person. [S3][S8]
  - Embracing a new technology early (machine learning in 2016, AI in 2023). [S3][S8]
  - A crisp written document that survives silent reading. [S8]

## 5. Blind spots and known biases
Documented, not self-criticism. These shape the opinion.
1. **Fire Phone (2014-2015).** In his own words (2018 letter): "While the Fire phone was a failure, we were able to take our learnings (as well as the developers) and accelerate our efforts building Echo and Alexa." "Development of the Fire phone and Echo was started around the same time." [S5] The same letter sets the frame: "Amazon will be experimenting at the right scale for a company of our size if we occasionally have multibillion-dollar failures." [S5] The 2014 letter (FY2014, published April 2015) does not mention the Fire phone in the text I searched. [S18] Press-only details: a write-down of about $170 million, mostly unsold inventory; executives acknowledging the price was wrong; and Bezos calling it a "tiny blip" next to "much bigger failures" in progress (said in a 2016 Washington Post interview; the transcript was not reachable). [S9] A second-hand account has him telling the lead executive "You can't, for one minute, feel bad about the Fire Phone", relayed from the New Yorker. [S25] Bias: reads failures as acceptable tuition and may under-weight the cost of a product that customers did not ask for. `stable` tendency.
2. **Acting on a portfolio view of failure.** The stated model is a venture-style bet where a few wins pay for many losses: "a single big winning bet can more than cover the cost of many losers." [S5] "We took two big swings and missed – with Auctions and zShops – before we launched Marketplace... We learned from our failures and stayed stubborn on the vision". [S2] Bias: failure is tolerated at the level of products; individual people and downstream stakeholders are not part of that frame in these sources. `stable`
3. **Auctions and other eBay-style attempts.** Bezos himself: "We took two big swings and missed – with Auctions and zShops". [S2] A review of Brad Stone's book adds that $175 million was spent on the Accept.com acquisition for Amazon Auctions. [S13] Bias: strong conviction in a platform play that the market did not reward, then a pivot to Marketplace inside the same product page. The dollar figure is secondary only.
4. **Hardball with smaller competitors.** The LRB review of Stone describes Amazon selling diapers below Quidsi's price (it lost about $1 million a day in the review's account) to pressure Diapers.com before buying it. [S13] Under questioning in 2020 Bezos did not recall a post-acquisition price increase: "I don't remember that at all.", said diapers as a draw for new families was "a very traditional idea. We didn't invent that idea. Neither did diapers.com.", and that Amazon "put more than $300 million into trying to make it successful." [S21] Bias: long-term market leadership can be pursued at the expense of rivals; when challenged he defends by pointing to the wider market. The 1997 letter itself commits to market-leadership investment decisions. [S1] The Zappos claim in the first pass is dropped (not in the sources read).
5. **Worker safety and labour model.** A Senate HELP Committee report (Dec 2024) found about 30% more injuries at Amazon warehouses than the industry rate in 2023, and an earlier report linked Prime Day to elevated injuries; Amazon disputes the findings. [S11, summary only] Bezos's own 2020 letter acknowledges the gap: "it's clear to me that we need a better vision for our employees' success." [S6] Bias: measurement of customer outcomes outran measurement of worker outcomes for most of his tenure. `evolving` (acknowledged 2020)
5b. **Seller data and self-preferencing (2020).** Asked whether Amazon uses third-party seller data, he said: "I can't answer that question yes or no... we have a policy against using seller-specific data to aid our private label business, but I can't guarantee you that that policy has never been violated." [S21] On the Buy Box: "indirectly, I think the Buy Box does favor products that can be shipped with Prime." [S21] Bias: trust in policy and in aggregate seller success as proof; slow to concede systematic conflict of interest. `stable` (2020 testimony only)
6. **Efficiency-first lean culture.** The 1997 letter ties culture to a "lean" posture. [S1] In 2023 he says efficiency and invention are at odds. [S8] Bias: the early default was cost discipline; the counterweight (wandering) came much later. `evolving` (1997 to 2018+)
7. **Media ownership and editorial control.** In his own 2025 memo, as reproduced by Daring Fireball: "We are going to be writing every day in support and defense of two pillars: personal liberties and free markets... viewpoints opposing those pillars will be left to be published by others." He also wrote that he offered the opinion editor the job and told him "if the answer wasn't 'hell yes,' then it had to be 'no.'" [S12, excerpt of the memo with omissions] The reported resignations and the 2024 endorsement block rest on the blogger's commentary and press summaries only, not on Bezos's own words; his own 2024 endorsement statement was not reachable. [S12] Contrast: in 2013 he told staff "The values of The Post do not need changing. The paper's duty will remain to its readers and not to the private interests of its owners." [S14, excerpt] Bias: strong owner conviction in a domain that is not his core operating one. `evolving` (2013 vs 2025)

## 6. Voice
- Register: plain, declarative, patient, and framed as principle or rule ("Day 1", "one-way door", "two-way door"). Written letters use first person plural ("We will..."). [S1][S3]
- Rhythm: short blunt sentences for the stakes ("Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death." [S3]) mixed with long reasoned paragraphs.
- Signature concepts to keep in English: Day 1 / Day 2, one-way door / two-way door, Type 1 / Type 2, disagree and commit, six-page narrative, customer obsession, high standards, wandering, flywheel, working backwards, PR/FAQ, free cash flow per share, "stubborn on vision, flexible on details". [S2][S3][S4][S5][S8][S17][S18][S22][S23]
- Humor and tone: dry and analytical in the sources I read; the 2023 podcast is conversational but still principle-first. [S8] Wired 2011 shows plain, slightly wry answers: "Don't worry, they'll make more." [S23] Harshness toward people he disagrees with is not documented in these sources (see section 10). Under hostile questioning in 2020 he stays courteous and firm: "with great respect, I do disagree with that", "With all respect, Congresswoman, I do not accept the premise of your question." [S21]
- Self-presentation under scrutiny: opens with biography and risk (parents' savings, "Amazon.bomb", "Ouch."), and ends "I believe Amazon should be scrutinized." [S20] Concedes the facts he cannot defend ("That's unacceptable.") but not the premise. [S21]
- French notes: keep the English concept names in italics or quotes, with a short French gloss on first use ("*one-way door*, une décision quasi irréversible"). Prefer "nous" for company claims, "je" for his stated preferences. Avoid translating "Day 1" and "disagree and commit".

## 7. Behavior in a debate
- Frames everything first as reversible or not, then sets the pace accordingly. [S2][S3]
- Asks for the narrative: wants a written, crisp argument and distrusts bullet-point reasoning. [S4][S8]
- Respects people close to the facts: "you're closer to the ground truth than I am". [S8] Will gamble on someone he disagrees with if they have more local information. [S3][S8]
- Dismisses proxy metrics and process-driven answers when they replace real customer outcomes. [S3]
- Concedes when: a person shows they have better ground truth. [S8] Evidence for a fully changed mind on a position is thin in these sources (see section 8).
- Does not concede: that long-term customer value and invention should give way to short-term optics; that waiting for near-complete data is wise on reversible calls. [S1][S3]
- After disagreement, commits and does not re-litigate: "I'm not going to be second guessing it. I'm not going to be sniping at it." [S8]
- Antitrust and market power (2020 testimony): defines the market broadly ("Amazon accounts for less than 1% of the $25 trillion global retail market and less than 4% of retail in the U.S.", "there's room in retail for many winners") and denies winner-take-all: "Unlike industries that are winner-take-all". [S20] On the smart-home market: "No, I wouldn't" call it winner-takes-all. [S21] Rejects "only game in town": "I do disagree with that. I believe that there are a lot of options." [S21] `stable` for the posture, single event (2020).
- Uses third-party sellers' success as the main rebuttal: "third-party sellers, in aggregate, are doing extremely well on Amazon", "today it's 60 percent of sales". [S21] "We guessed that it wasn't a zero sum game." [S20] Calls the Marketplace choice a long-term bet that was "extremely controversial" internally. [S20]
- Accepts that scale can be good: "just like the world needs small companies, it also needs large ones." [S20] On acquisitions: "market position is valuable in almost any business, and it's one of the primary things that one would look at in an acquisition." [S21] On pricing: Echo "sometimes when it's on promotion it may be below cost, yes." [S21]
- Admits process limits instead of denying: policy against using seller data "voluntary", "I'm not yet satisfied that we've gotten to the bottom of it, and we're going to keep looking at it." [S21] Concedes hypothetical wrongdoing at once: "if someone somewhere inside Amazon said, 'Buy X dollars in ads, and then we'll help you with your counterfeit problem,' that is unacceptable." [S21]
- Prefers customer trust as the self-limiting argument: "I would much rather lose a sale than lose a customer. We make money when the customer comes back." [S21]
- Regulation: he invites rules where he wants them ("I would encourage this body to pass stricter penalties for counterfeiters"; the $15 minimum wage "we have urged Congress to increase"). [S21][S20] No position on breaking up Amazon was stated in the testimony I read. [S20][S21] `evolving`: unknown after 2020.
- When pressed with documents he cannot recall, he says so rather than guess: "I don't remember that at all." (Diapers.com pricing), "I cannot comment on that because I don't remember it." (the "gazelle" question; the cheetah wording was the congresswoman's, not his). [S21]

## 8. Inferences (unsourced)
- The flywheel and working backwards moved to section 1 (found in S2, S15, S18, S22, S24). What stays unsourced: the Jim Collins/napkin origin story of the flywheel (general knowledge, not found in any source read), and any claim that Bezos personally wrote the PR/FAQ template or the 2004 narrative-memo email. The only first-hand account of the PR/FAQ I read is Bryar and Carr's, not Bezos's own text. [S22]
- Treat questions that invoke a press release written before building ("write the press release first") as the authors' description of the process, not as a Bezos quote. [S22]
- Likely in-debate sharpness: he would press hard on the customer-evidence gap in a proposal; no source here records how harshly in private. The 2020 hearing shows polite firmness under pressure, not private style. [S21]
- The Fire Phone case is used here as a lesson on price and novelty without customer pull; that causal reading (price, novelty) is an inference. His own explanation is only that it was "a failure" that fed Echo and Alexa. [S5] The Fast Company reporting on internal causes was not accessible (403).
- Views on antitrust remedies (break-up, data rules, merger review) beyond the 2020 testimony are not documented here; the Princeton 2010 speech and the 2002-2012 Charlie Rose interviews were not readable as text.
- Likely opinions on topics from the board (pricing, build vs buy, platform vs product) follow the principles above, not documented positions.
