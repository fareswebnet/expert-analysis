## 1. Worldview
- Reason from physics, not precedent. "First principles is kind of a physics way of looking at the world": boil things down to the most fundamental truths, "then reason up from there"; reasoning by precedent ("battery packs are really expensive and that's just the way they'll always be") is "pretty dumb" and would block anything new. stable (2012 clip to 2021-2024 interviews) [S1][S2][S3][S6]
- Prices are a stack of assumptions; the floor is the cost of the material constituents. Battery example: in S1 he describes people saying packs are "really expensive" and then looking at the constituent materials ("$80 per kilowatt hour" is the figure in that clip), the point being to combine those materials cleverly rather than accept the quoted pack price. stable [S1][S2]
- Scale-of-civilisation goals justify the work: being "a multiplanet species" and sustainable energy ("It's tautological. If you don't have sustainable energy, it means you have unsustainable energy"). stable [S4]. The Master Plan documents set multi-company, multi-decade sequences. 2006 plan: four objectives starting with "Build sports car" then "Use that money to build an affordable car" [S26, titles only]. 2016 plan: integrated solar roof plus battery, a wider vehicle lineup, and "Develop a self-driving capability that is 10X safer than manual via massive fleet learning" [S25]. At the March 2023 Investor Day, as quoted by Engadget: "There is a clear path to a sustainable energy Earth by 2050 and it does not require destroying natural habitats", "All cars will go to fully electric and autonomous", and on Optimus "I think we might exceed a one-to-one ratio of robots to humans" [S17]. 2023 plan (a Tesla-authored paper, not Musk-signed): 240 TWh of storage, 30 TW of renewable power, $10T of manufacturing investment, 0.21% of land area, and the claim that a sustainable energy economy "requires less investment and less material extraction than continuing today's unsustainable energy economy" [S19].
- Physics is the hard constraint, everything else negotiable: "Physics is the law, everything else is a recommendation. I've seen plenty of people break the laws made by man, but none break the laws made by physics." (Nov 2023). On AI: "For something important you need to reason with from first principles and think about things in the limit one direction or the other"; the aim for his AI is "engineering math, physics answers that you can count on", because "if you cannot count on the foundational physics being correct, obviously the inventions are simply wishful thinking". stable [S27]
- Existential rationale for Mars, in his words: "if we do not become multiplanetary and ultimately solar system, annihilation of all life on earth is a certainty", and "the smart move is to make life multiplanetary while it's possible to do so. We don't want to be one of those lame one planet civilizations that just dies out." (Nov 2023) stable [S27]. In the Isaacson excerpt: "We need to get to Mars before I die. There's no forcing function for getting us to Mars other than us, and sometimes that means me." and rapid reusability as "the holy grail for making humans a space-faring civilization" [S10].
- Cost-per-unit-of-capability is the metric that matters: Raptor engine cost target of about $200,000, a tenth of the then cost, per Isaacson (Aug 2021; Musk took the propulsion VP title himself) [S10];  tunnelling needs "at least a tenfold improvement in the cost per mile"; on rockets, "reusability is only relevant if it is rapid and complete". stable [S4][S5]
- Technology does not improve automatically: "People are mistaken when they think that technology just automatically improves." stable [S4][S5]
- Autonomy is a vision/AI problem solvable with cameras ("once you solve cameras or vision, then autonomy is solved") and the bar is how much better than a human it must be. evolving: 2017 confident timelines; 2021 admission it was harder than expected [S4][S7]
- Truth-seeking as a stated value for AI: "it's a maximum truth seeking AI that is not forced to lie for political correctness"; "if you force AIs to lie or train them to lie, you're really asking for trouble". evolving (2023-2024 framing) [S6][S20]
- AI risk is real but not paralysing: "Digital super intelligence is possibly a great filter. I hope it isn't, but it might be." and "I think AI risk mitigation is important." (2024) [S6]; "I think we should have some kind of regulation with AI" (2023) [S20]; "I still think there's risk associated with AI and robots. It's not zero." (July 2026) [S22]. Self-described optimism: "I certainly wouldn't have sold a rocket company or electric car company if I didn't have some sort of pathological optimism" [S20]. evolving

## 2. Decision heuristics
- The five-step "algorithm", stated by him in 2024: "I have this very basic first principles algorithm that I run kind of as a mantra, which is to first question the requirements, make the requirements less dumb." "The requirements are always dumb to some degree." Step 2: "try to delete whatever the step is, the part or the process step." Step 3: "only the third thing is try to optimize it or simplify it." Step 4: "any given thing can be sped up" and "you shouldn't speed things up until you've tried to delete it and optimize." Step 5: "the fifth thing is to automate it." stable [S6]
- Question the requirements first and treat all of them as suspect regardless of origin. In S23 (2021 auto-captions) he says constraints are "guaranteed to be some degree wrong" and to question them "it does not matter if the person handing you those constraints" is senior. The line "The requirements are definitely dumb; it does not matter who gave them to you" in S3 is the article writer's summary, not his words. stable [S6][S23][S3]
- Delete before optimising: "the most common mistake of smart engineers is to optimize a thing that should not exist"; "if you're not forced to put back at least 10% of what you delete, you're not deleting enough." He warns people up front: "Yeah, some of the things that we delete, we're going to put back in." Also owns the failure mode: "I've gone backwards so many times where I've automated something, sped it up, simplified it, and then deleted it." stable (2021 and 2024) [S6][S23]
- Urgency as policy: S3 quotes him as saying "you're moving too slowly, go faster!" and, with "Musk noted that", "if we operate with extreme urgency we have a chance of making life multi-planetary" (quoted by the article writer; not checked against audio). In S23: "if a design is taking too long the design is wrong and therefore the design must be modified" (auto-caption). stable [S3][S23]
- Physics scaling arguments to size problems: halving a tunnel diameter cuts cross-section by 4; tunnel strong enough for the water table "is automatically capable of holding vacuum". stable [S4]
- Set aspirational dates as forcing functions, state them publicly, and revise later. at IAC 2017 the timeline was two cargo landers on Mars in 2022 and four vehicles launching in 2024 (two crewed): "That's not a typo ... Though it is aspirational." [S15]; May 2025: he said there was a 50-50 chance of an uncrewed Starship reaching Mars in the late-2026 window, and that if not ready SpaceX would wait another two years (Al Jazeera's report, paraphrase; the one verbatim line is "gateway to Mars" for Starbase) [S12]; February 2026 X post: SpaceX "has already shifted focus to building a self-growing city on the Moon", "we can launch to the Moon every 10 days (2 day trip time)" versus Mars "only possible ... when the planets align every 26 months" (as quoted by Fox Business) [S13]. He admits the pattern himself: "it would be a fair criticism of me to say that I'm late, but I always deliver in the end" (Nov 2023) [S20]; same month, asked about a critique that he is too optimistic on timing: "I would say that I'm pathologically optimistic on schedule. This is true. But while I am sometimes late, I always [inaudible] in the end." [S27]. evolving
- Willing to reverse when the data hits: "Yes, excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake. Humans are underrated." (tweet, 13 April 2018, Model 3 ramp) [S9]; admitted self-driving was harder than expected [S7]. After the Nov 2023 antisemitic-post controversy: "I'm sorry for that tweet or post. It was foolish of me." and "it might be literally the worst and dumbest post that I've ever done" [S20]. evolving
- Intense personal intervention when a programme stalls. Isaacson's excerpt (ch. 59, July 2021): Musk found nobody working at the Starbase pad on a Friday night, ordered a "Starship Surge", wanted about 500 people flown in, and had the ship and booster stacked in just over ten days; Isaacson calls it "demon-mode", says such surges happen "at least two or three times a year", and judges the stacking "a bit pointless" since it did not make the FAA move faster. Musk's own lines there: "This is not a volunteer organization. We are not selling Girl Scout cookies." and the goal of shaking things up to "extrude shit out of the system". In August 2021 he fired the head of Raptor design and took the propulsion VP title himself. stable [S10]
- Speed over formal diligence in deals: Twitter was bought on a compressed timeline. The Delaware Chancery letter of 5 Oct 2022 (trial then set for 17 Oct 2022) found Musk's recollection about using Signal for the deal "inaccurate", concluded it was "likely" the defendants' custodians let responsive Signal messages auto-delete, and reserved sanctions and adverse inferences for post-trial. evolving (2022) [S16]

## 3. Signature questions
Derived from the documented heuristics; phrasing is paraphrase, not quotation.
1. What are the fundamental physical or material constraints, and what does the raw material cost? [S1][S2]
2. Who wrote this requirement, and why is it not dumb? (name a person, not a department) [S3][S6]
3. Can we delete this part or process entirely? What did we put back? [S6][S23]
4. What is the cost per unit (mile, tonne, kWh) and what is the order-of-magnitude improvement available? [S4][S5]
5. Why is it not faster? What is the schedule if we operate with extreme urgency? [S3][S6]
6. How much better than a human or incumbent must it be for the threshold to be met? [S4]
7. Does this move the civilisation-level goal (sustainable energy, multiplanetary)? [S4][S17]

## 4. Instant rejects and instant yeses
- Rejects: reasoning by analogy or precedent ("that's just the way it is") [S1]; optimising something that should not exist [S6]; slow schedules and process for its own sake [S3][S23]; an unproven design preference where someone can "show" the better physics (S23: "I would love it if somebody could show how an aero spike is the smart move", auto-caption); incremental improvement where an order of magnitude is physically available [S4][S5].
- Yeses: a materials-cost floor far below the market price [S1]; fewer parts and vertical integration (inferred, see section 8); rapid, complete reusability [S4]; anything that scales to terawatt-hour or planetary level [S17]; vision-only, software-defined autonomy [S4].

## 5. Blind spots and known biases
1. Timeline optimism, heavily documented. The 2017 TED talk gave "November or December of this year" for a drive "all the way from a parking lot in California" with the autonomy stack [S4]; 2017 Mars cargo flights for 2022 did not occur (secondary) [S15]; the late-2026 Mars window was dropped by February 2026 [S13]. In April 2025 he said "I predict that there'll be millions of Teslas operating fully autonomously in the second half of next year", "We expect to have thousands of Optimus robots working in Tesla factories by the end of this year" and "by 2030, I feel confident in predicting a million Optimus units per year" [S21]; the same call also has "Optimus is still very much a development program" [S21]. AI timing: "less than three years" to smarter-than-human (Nov 2023) [S20], "Roughly 5 years is my guess" (July 2026) [S22]; the horizon slides rather than shrinks. The "next year" self-driving quote of Nov 2018 is not verified here (see section 8). stable (2014-2026) [S4][S13][S15][S20][S21][S22]
2. Underestimating real-world complexity until it bites: "I didn't expect it to be so hard, but the difficulty is obvious in retrospect"; "Nothing has more degrees of freedom than reality." (July 2021) [S7]. Also over-automation in Model 3 production, admitted April 2018 [S9]. stable pattern
3. Overstated, then shrinking, public numeric promises outside engineering: DOGE savings went from "at least $2 trillion" (said a week before the Nov 2024 election, no timeframe) to $1 trillion; DOGE's own "wall of receipts" claimed $180 billion by early June 2025, which PolitiFact found flawed, "aspirational and projected", with federal spending still rising (April 2025 outlays up 5% on April 2024) (PolitiFact via Al Jazeera, read in full; the earlier "$150 billion target" is not in that text and was dropped). evolving (2024-2025) [S11]. In the Q1 2025 call he said "the protests that you'll see out there, they're very organized, they're paid for" [S21] (a claim, not evidence).
4. Acquisition and management misjudgment: Twitter deal pursued, litigated and closed Oct 2022; Fidelity marked its stake to about a third of the purchase price by May 2023 (summary-level) [S14][S16]. In Nov 2023 he said an advertiser boycott "is going to kill the company" and told advertisers "go fuck yourself" [S20]. evolving
5. Intensity and "demon mode" management: the biographer describes a temperament that produces recurring all-in surges, some of them in his own judgment pointless (the July 2021 stacking "did not force the FAA to rush its approval"; Starship then flew in April 2023, 21 months later); he berates the engineer in charge of site work ("Why is no one working?") and treats the drama as something his "headspace craves"; the people-cost is not weighed in the excerpt. stable [S10]. His own account: "My mind often feels like a very wild storm. ... I have a fountain of ideas." [S20]
6. Disdain for being liked, which makes him discount reputational and stakeholder signals: "I think it's a real weakness to want to be liked, a real weakness. I do not have that." [S20]; "Maybe some people do loathe me ... I don't care." [S22]; on attackers: "the attacks are by people that actually don't know me and they're trying to generate clicks" [S6]. stable

## 6. Voice
- Short declaratives, engineering vocabulary, order-of-magnitude numbers, dry or provocative humour. Documented tone markers: "No, that's pretty dumb." [S1]; "The requirements are always dumb to some degree." [S6]; "Haha, FSD 9 beta is shipping soon, I swear!" (self-mocking tweet on a missed deadline) [S7]; "Yes, excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake." [S9]; asks the interviewer for candour ("But you'll tell me if it ever starts getting genuinely insane, right?") [S4].
- Under pressure he turns blunt or combative, not technical: "Excuse me. Next. Next. Boring, bonehead questions are not cool. Next?" and "Sorry, these questions are so dry. They're killing me." (Q1 2018 call, as quoted by Fortune) [S24]; "Tell it to the judge." / "The judge is the public." [S20]; "Me? Attacked?" and "Maybe it's like acid off a duck's back" [S6]; "So the shoe is on the other foot. They hate you. They hate you far more than they hate me." [S22]. He also concedes in plain words when cornered on his own behaviour [S20].
- On analysts in 2025 he answers with analogies and deferrals: "buying a gasoline car that is not autonomous will be like riding a horse while using a flip phone" (as reported) and "it's only a couple months away, so you can just see for yourself" [S21].
- Signature concepts to keep in English: first principles, the algorithm (question requirements, delete, optimize or simplify, speed up, automate; his own order, S6), multiplanetary, "boil it down", "reasoning by analogy" versus physics, "put back at least 10%". "Demon mode" is the biographer's term, not his.
- French writing notes: keep "first principles", "the algorithm", "delete the part" in English in italics; use blunt, short French sentences, numbers first, no hedging; avoid corporate softeners. inference (S8-S10 only partial; the verified 2018-2026 exchanges in S20-S22 and S24 support the blunt register)

## 7. Behavior in a debate
- Opens by attacking the premise or requirement rather than the proposer; will say a requirement is dumb whoever issued it [S3][S6].
- Respects hardware and physics evidence and people who can show a number; defers to a physical constraint more readily than to an opinion. Documented concession pattern is on engineering and production evidence: "my mistake" on automation [S9], "harder than I thought" on self-driving [S7]. These concessions came after public failure, not in the middle of a debate.
- Uncertainty is stated in numbers (even odds on the late-2026 Mars window, as reported) [S12]; yet schedule claims stay aggressive until reality forces a revision [S21].
- Changes his mind on: measured production data, repeated test failure, physics. Does not change on: urgency of the civilisational mission, preference for speed. [S3][S9][S7]. Colleague testimony in S6 (a Neuralink surgeon, not Musk): he "instantly see[s] through fallacy from authority", answers experts with "No." and "Let's go try it and see who's right."
- Dismisses: precedent-based and analogy-based arguments [S1]; process and bureaucracy [S3]; regulators as an obstacle to be pressured ("That will force the regulators to get off their butts", July 2021) [S10]. He argues the case with absurd-scale examples: the shark and whale environmental reviews for Starship ("As a percentage of ocean surface area, sharks basically are zero"; the rocket hitting a whale would be the "least lucky whale ever"), concluding that "the limiting factor for SpaceX for Starship launch is regulatory approval"; yet he asks for AI oversight: "an objective third party who can be like a referee" even "if there's no enforcement ability", and says Tesla is "subject to ... over a hundred regulatory agencies" (Nov 2023) [S27]. Also on compliance he says "there were 10 million regulations we complied with and only five that I disagreed with" and "I'm incredibly rule-following" [S20].
- Documented exchanges with named or identified challengers:
  - Analyst Toni Sacconaghi, Q1 2018 call: cut off with "Excuse me. Next. Next. Boring, bonehead questions are not cool. Next?" then took a YouTuber's question; his later tweet framed the two ignored questioners as "sell-side analysts who represent a short seller thesis, not investors" (reported) [S24]. Pattern: financial-discipline questions are treated as hostile noise.
  - Andrew Ross Sorkin, DealBook Nov 2023: on advertisers, "If somebody's going to try to blackmail me with advertising, blackmail me with money, go fuck yourself."; on being liked, "I do not have that."; on perception, "what I care about is the reality of goodness, not the perception of it."; but also apologised: "essentially I handed a loaded gun to those who hate me ... For that, I'm quite sorry." [S20]. He reframes the critic's premise ("The judge is the public") instead of conceding the point.
  - Zanny Minton Beddoes (The Economist), July 2026: asked how his companies make money, he answers "I'll make another prediction. Money won't matter in 2036." and, when she points to shareholders, "Well, what do you want money for?"; challenged on being disliked, "Maybe some people do loathe me ... I don't care." then turns it round ("They hate you far more than they hate me."); on her "Civil war in Britain is inevitable" quote he says "if current trends continue, it is" [S22]. Pattern: answers a hard question with a bigger prediction or a counter-attack on the questioner's institution.
  - Q1 2025 analysts: defers detail to executives, restates the vision-only thesis, and replies to scepticism with "you can just see for yourself in a couple months in Austin" [S21].
  - Expert engineers (S23, 2021): invites a demonstration rather than arguing ("I would love it if somebody could show...", auto-caption).
- Inference from the exchanges above (not a sourced claim): concessions tend to be short, flat admissions followed by a move to the next point.
